Social policy · Upstream prevention

Fire on the Mountain

How far up are we willing to meet people?

By Adekunle OmonihiReflection

Every society has a mountain to climb. We can invest in people on the way up, or meet them later in emergency departments, shelters, courtrooms and prisons.

I have lived in Canada for three years, but I spent most of my life in Nigeria. That experience shapes the way I think about prevention.

In Nigeria, I watched insecurity become more than a security problem. Armed attacks and extortion have pushed farmers away from their land, threatening production and adding to pressure on food prices. But I want to be careful with the lesson.

Nigeria's insecurity has many causes. It would be wrong to draw a straight line from children outside formal schooling—or from the Almajiri system—to banditry or extremism. The evidence is more nuanced: poverty, exclusion, limited opportunity and weak protection can create vulnerabilities that violent groups exploit. They do not predetermine a child's future. That is the warning I carry into Canada.

A social problem does not remain somebody else's problem forever.

Recent U.S. “teen takeover” videos offer another caution. Some gatherings have become violent, but reporting also shows that the label covers very different events, many largely non-violent, and that some young people describe a shortage of safe, welcoming places to gather. The useful question is not, “What is wrong with this generation?”

What conditions are we creating around them?

Canada already has a language for this. Public-health guidance on youth substance use calls for comprehensive school approaches combining upstream prevention, harm reduction, stigma reduction and equity. That suggests practical choices: school social workers with time to build relationships; family supports before conflict becomes crisis; recreation and employment pathways; and well-designed peer programmes that help young people connect with trusted adults and professional care.

Peer support is not magic, and it cannot replace parents, teachers, counsellors or clinicians. Some programmes have produced encouraging results, while broader evidence remains uneven and implementation quality matters. The same principle should guide Canada's drug-policy debate. “Harm reduction versus recovery” becomes a false choice when people need different supports at different stages of their lives.

Aṣa has said Fire on the Mountain grew from a question she and Cobhams Asuquo were asking themselves: “What can we contribute? How can we help?” I hear that question as a challenge to social policy, too.

How far up the mountain are we willing to meet people?

Accountability still matters. Public safety still matters.

But so does timing.

We can keep getting better at catching people at the bottom.

Or we can become just as serious about reaching them before the fall.
